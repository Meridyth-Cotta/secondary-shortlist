How the process works · briefing from the Year 5 parents' meeting
The state system
  • One form, six choices. All London state applications go through the Pan-London system on a single common application form. Use all six preferences.
  • Apply through the borough you live in, not the borough the school sits in.
  • Schools never see your ranking. Each ranks every applicant against its own published criteria. The borough then offers the highest-ranked school on your list that can give your child a place.
  • One offer only, on National Offer Day. If none of the six can offer, the council allocates whatever has space.
  • Only the form counts. Certificates, awards and enrichment carry no weight, because schools must treat every applicant equally.
Supplementary forms
  • Church schools need a separate supplementary form that goes back to the school, not the borough. Without it your child drops into a lower priority group.
  • Aptitude routes need their own form too, with deadlines well before 31 October.
  • Arrange church signatures early. If one is delayed, submit the form anyway and follow up.
Ranking and safety nets
  • Be ambitious, but keep safety nets. Count how many criteria your child actually meets at each school. If the answer is none, think hard before spending a preference on it.
  • Keep state schools on the form even if aiming private. Circumstances change and fees can stop being possible.
  • Grammar results arrive before the deadline, so you can rank knowing the score. A preference for a grammar he has not reached the qualifying score for is a wasted slot.
  • Stay on waiting lists. Places move well into September.
  • Appeals rarely succeed. Admissions are run strictly, so there is usually no procedural error to challenge.
Entrance exams
  • Grammar schools test verbal reasoning, non-verbal reasoning and maths.
  • Independent schools test maths and English, with English the priority, because strong English is read as access to the whole curriculum.
  • Comprehension papers are long. Wide reading is the preparation that works.
  • Writing is judged on the opening. Examiners typically form a view from the first two or three paragraphs. Rehearsed pieces adapted to the title are transparent to schools and risky if they do not answer the question set.
  • Tests are moving earlier. Top grammars now test in September of Year 6, and some areas have shifted to the end of Year 5.
  • Plan the running order. Six or seven exams leaves children exhausted. Let early ones act as practice and avoid making the top choice the last.
Visiting
  • Visit during the school day where you can, not only evening events, to see real lessons and how staff and pupils interact.
  • Three visits out of school at most, on different days. Visit the rest on your own.
  • Year 5 advice: see two or three this year and the rest next year, so visits are not crowded into one autumn.
  • On the day, talk to pupils, watch the teaching, and look at the standard of work on display. Make notes straight afterwards.
Choosing
  • Put the child first, not the prestige. Choose where your child will be happy.
  • Be realistic about the commute. Ninety minutes each way is too much at eleven, and after the clocks change children travel home in the dark.
  • Light tutoring is fine. Heavy tutoring produces children who struggle to think independently in the exam and cannot keep up once admitted.
